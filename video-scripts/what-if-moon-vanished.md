## Script

**[ON SCREEN: black. A full Moon fades in over a night ocean. It cuts out. Black.]**

**HOOK (0:00)**
You would not die if the Moon vanished tomorrow. So why would your nights get a hundred times darker, and your tides lose two thirds of their size?

**[ON SCREEN: Moon at 384,400 km from Earth, a light-beam icon crossing the gap. Counter: 1.3 s.]**

**THE TURN (0:11)**
Let's delete the Moon and watch what actually happens, in order. Not the movie version. The real one. And I'll warn you now: the worst part isn't tomorrow. It's what happens over the next ten million years.

**BEAT 1: The first 1.3 seconds (0:26)**
The Moon is about 384,000 kilometres away. Light takes 1.3 seconds to cross that gap, and so does any change in gravity. So for 1.3 seconds, everyone on the night side is still looking at a Moon that is already gone.

**[ON SCREEN: night-side street scene; the Moon icon blinks out.]**

**BEAT 2: The first night (0:42)**
Then it goes out. A full Moon gives roughly a quarter of a lux of light. A moonless, clear night is closer to two thousandths. That is about a hundred times darker. You would see more stars than you have ever seen from your own street. And you would not see your own garden.

**[ON SCREEN: split image, same street, full-Moon night vs starlight only; light-meter bar 0.25 vs 0.002 lux.]**

**BEAT 2B: The upside (1:04)**
There is one group who would be thrilled: astronomers. Moonlight washes out faint galaxies, nebulae and meteors. Moon-free nights are the best nights for deep-sky work, and they only come around once a month. With no Moon, every clear night would be one of them. Meteor showers would stop competing with a bright Moon. The sky you lose in light, you gain in depth.

**[ON SCREEN: split: washed-out sky with a bright Moon vs the same sky full of stars; a meteor streak.]**

**BEAT 3: The animals that use it (1:30)**
For a lot of animals, the Moon is not decoration. It is a tool. Dung beetles roll their dung balls in straight lines at night, steering by the pattern of polarised light around the Moon. Take it away and they have to rely on the dimmer glow of the Milky Way. In lab tests, they can. But they are slower, and they get it wrong more often.

And coral on the Great Barrier Reef spawns together, a few nights after the full Moon in late spring. Remove the Moon and the cue is gone. Nobody knows when to release.

**[ON SCREEN: dung beetle clip or animation; a reef with a moon-phase calendar and the spawning window highlighted.]**

**BEAT 4: The tides, hour by hour (2:09)**
Now the oceans. And this is the first thing every disaster video gets wrong: no tsunami. The Moon does not hold the ocean in place. It stretches it. Take that stretch away and the water does not rush out. It relaxes.

The Sun also pulls on the oceans, but only about half as hard as the Moon does. With the Moon gone, tides would settle at roughly a third of their size. High tide would still happen. It just would not reach as far.

**[ON SCREEN: animated coast; tide line retreating from high mark to a lower mark; label "about 1/3".]**

**BEAT 5: What the tides were doing (2:43)**
Tides shape coasts. Tidal pools, mudflats and estuaries are nurseries for fish and shellfish. Shrink the tide and those zones shrink with it. Coastal ecosystems would have to move, or die back, over decades.

**[ON SCREEN: tidal flat at high vs low water, with a marked band showing the shrinking range.]**

**BEAT 5B: What people lose (2:56)**
Humans built around the tide, too. Big ships time their arrival at some ports to high water. Coastal power plants harvest the tide: the Rance plant in France has been running since 1966, producing around 240 megawatts at its peak. Shrink the tide and the output shrinks with it.

And we named our time after the Moon. The word "month" comes from "moon". Monday is Moon-day. The old calendars were lunar. Take the Moon away and the oldest clock we ever had stops.

**[ON SCREEN: container ship in a tidal port; the Rance plant; a calendar with Moon phases fading out.]**

**BEAT 5C: What would NOT change (3:30)**
Before we go on, a quick myth check. People blame the Moon for a lot. More births on full Moon nights. More emergency visits. Stranger behaviour. Researchers have gone through hospital records looking for these links, and across the large studies, they mostly find nothing reliable. So if you were hoping the Moon vanishing would calm the world down, I have bad news. People would be exactly as strange as they are now. Only the sky would be different.

**[ON SCREEN: hospital-chart style graphic with a flat line; "full Moon" label on a spike that isn't there.]**

**RECAP (4:01)**
So far: darker nights, confused animals, smaller tides. Annoying. Not an apocalypse. So where is the actual danger? It's in two places. One is the sky. One is the planet itself.

**BEAT 6: The sky: eclipses end (4:14)**
The Sun is about four hundred times wider than the Moon. And it is about four hundred times farther away. That is why, from Earth, the two look almost the same size, and why the Moon can cover the Sun exactly in a total eclipse. That coincidence is rare among the planets we know.

It's also how we learned things. In 1868, astronomers watching an eclipse saw a spectral line nobody could match. It was a new element, helium, found in the Sun before anyone found it on Earth.

Without the Moon, no more eclipses. The Moon is also drifting away from us by about 3.8 centimetres a year, so total eclipses will end anyway, hundreds of millions of years from now. You would just lose the whole run of them at once.

**[ON SCREEN: Sun and Moon at equal apparent size, with "400 x" labels; eclipse corona; 1868 spectrum sketch.]**

**BEAT 7: Your day, quietly (5:07)**
The Moon also drags on Earth's spin. It is slowing us down, adding about two milliseconds to the day every century. Remove the Moon and that braking stops. The day stops growing. Nobody would feel it. But it shows the timescale we are now on. Not weeks. Not years. Geology.

**[ON SCREEN: clock face, 24:00:00.002, "per century" label.]**

**BEAT 8: The planet: the tilt (5:27)**
Here is the real danger. Earth leans over by about 23 degrees. That tilt gives you seasons. The Moon acts like a stabiliser, so the lean barely wanders. Models from the 1990s suggested that, without the Moon, the tilt could swing from nearly upright to nearly lying on its side, over millions of years. Newer models say the swings may be smaller. But even a swing of ten or twenty degrees would redraw the climate zones.

An upright Earth has almost no seasons. A tilted-over Earth has a summer sun that never sets. Neither is something the life we have now was built for.

**[ON SCREEN: Earth with a tilt arrow wobbling; seasons bands shifting on a globe; a split of "no tilt" vs "tipped over" climates.]**

**BEAT 9: Why we even have one (6:08)**
And here's the strange part. We aren't supposed to have a Moon this big. About 4.5 billion years ago, a Mars-sized body hit the young Earth. The debris merged into the Moon. For a rocky planet, our Moon is unusually large next to its planet, and that is part of why it stabilises us so well.

It's also a time capsule. With no air and almost no erosion, the Moon keeps a record of the impacts that hit this part of the solar system. Without it, we'd lose the clearest record of our own early history.

**[ON SCREEN: impact animation, Theia hitting early Earth; Moon surface with a crater timeline.]**

**BEAT 10: The next step (6:47)**
And one more thing we'd lose: our first stop. Apollo reached the Moon in about three days. Twelve people have walked on it. Every plan for going further, from lunar bases to refuelling stations, starts there, because it is the only other world close enough to reach in days, not months. Without it, our first step into space has nowhere to land.

**[ON SCREEN: Apollo footprint; a lunar-base concept; Mars on a long arrow.]**

**THE PAYOFF (7:12)**
So here is what the Moon's disappearance really does. Tomorrow: dark nights, smaller tides, a very strange sky. In ten thousand years: coasts rearranged. In ten million: a climate with no fixed pattern.

And one thing does not change at all. Earth's path around the Sun. The Moon was never what kept us in orbit. It was what kept us steady.

**[ON SCREEN: Earth's orbit line around the Sun, unchanged, with a moon-shaped gap beside it.]**

**THE CLOSE (7:36)**
If you want the other half, what happens if the Sun's gravity vanished instead, that video is next.

**[END SCREEN: next video.]**

## Facts to verify before you record
- Tides: Sun's effect is roughly 46% of the Moon's, so tides fall to about 30% of today's. Say "about a third".
- Axial tilt: Laskar et al. (1993) show large swings; Lissauer et al. (2012) found smaller ones. Keep the hedge.
- Day length: about 2 ms per century, long-term average. Moon recession: about 3.8 cm per year.
- Lux figures are approximate: full Moon about 0.25 lux, starlight only about 0.002 lux.
- Coral spawning: Great Barrier Reef, several nights after the full Moon in late spring.
- Dung beetles: Dacke et al. (2003, 2013) on lunar polarised light and Milky Way orientation. Phrase the "slower, more errors" line to match what you read in the papers.
- Eclipses: Sun about 400x the Moon's diameter and about 400x as far. Helium line seen at the 1868 eclipse. "Hundreds of millions of years" for the end of total eclipses is a commonly cited estimate; check your source.
- Rance tidal plant (France): operating since 1966, about 240 MW peak. Check current figures.
- Full-moon myths: large studies of births and ER visits mostly find no reliable effect. Cite a specific review if you name one.
- Apollo transit: about three days. Twelve people have walked on the Moon.
- Giant impact about 4.5 billion years ago. "Unusually large for a rocky planet" is accurate for Earth's Moon; Pluto-Charon is a larger ratio but Pluto is a dwarf planet.
